From Antinovelė · Work in progress (2026)

A village on the map, yet no village at all, only an asphalt lane through the forest where the dirt road used to be, leading to a single new homestead; across the whole length of the lake a young man rows in a Helly Hansen jacket and ski hat, a colourful toy assembled out of Lego pieces; population census in the Tsarist church registers, 1905. Population: zero.

Facsimiles in the parish books, I inspected the registers, the priest’s signature. The water is black as a mirror; under the water, they say, lies a sunken bell; others say a German tank with its entire crew tried to cross and went through the ice after the war.

Through forests over green moss and along wild-boar trails, hour after hour he strides through ancestral ground, across sprouting farmers’ fields and sandy country roads, skirting Gaidelių Dvarelis, following boar fords across streams toward the capital; legend says Mažvydas walked like this with a bundle from Samogitia – the traveller snickers to himself at the thought, drying his boots and changing socks on a size-13 foot, registering over fifty kilometres that those extra one or two centimetres of stride really do add enough ground to shrink the map’s nine hours down toward half an hour.

From Antinovelė · Work in progress (2026)

In a winter homeless shelter, between charity dinners, you read Pranic Nourishment – Nutrition for the New Millennium, a book about transitioning from solid food to living on prana.

In a holistic colon-hydrotherapy clinic, Gregorian chant plays. Outside the window the wind bends tall trees; right in the windowpane, blue glass dolphins hang motionless.

* * *

Head pressed against thick stone walls and blackened gates with an iron handle roughly braided by a blacksmith a century ago, you hammer like a Thai fighter through bricks until you feel bone cracks in your hands. Behind them in the dark: mute, invisible, vacuum-breathing atoms of the guards. Walls over three metres, from back when Gutenberg chased hens between wooden tables in his father’s furrier-tavern among unwashed bearded men.

A tiny cell. A little window deep in the wall. Wooden bed, small chair, sink, slop bucket. You remember Van Gogh’s painting. Where is that miniature pinhole in the wall, who is standing there in the secret corridor? That ache of absence, or arthritis, you no longer know what is yours, who you are, what is experience, in whose solemnity. You kneel, aloud, as if recording into a hidden microphone, like in the cell with the Syrian next to you in for weapons, not 3 kilos of coke, in the cell with you fardi masaker. With your own voice you out-pray yourself until the public-order guards announce evening roll-call over the loudspeaker.

* * *

In the kitchenette where you burned a pot, political science students with national interests find a few minutes for oratorical practice: argument is treason.

Don’t cry, artificial intelligence tells me, speak so it shows what is happening instead of naming emotions. Some part of you screams at the bottom of the well: wake up, open your eyes, look at the screen and fill yourself.

* * *

A large windowless room, flat white light. Healthy, calm men. Smiling. You ask for the drug. They keep smiling. Neither refusing nor giving. Ecological and economical, exactly what is needed.

Next time, a man with a knife. You walk toward him. Nobody there. The knife was chemistry, the man was chemistry, the fear yours. Pain will not save you. There is a species that never comes to fruition, reconciled to the fate other species assign them.

You drank what the forest people drink. Ate what grows on dung after rain. Knelt on a carpet smelling of feet and rosewater. Dreamed ahead without asking.

* * *

Still dark. You fold the blanket corners in. No note.

By the gate the student is smoking. Where will you go. Ten years you dreamed only of yourself. Now you want to dream far.

You cannot return to us, two-year deportation, they kept apologising. The gate does not creak, oiled, from afar walked through the night the Dreamer.

* * *

Selected lines · Lithuanian original and English rendering

Paskui laivais į už Atlanto. Išdrąskė gyvenimus, tie, kas liko, pražuvo, tie kas išvažiavo, pamiršo.

Then by ship, across the Atlantic. It tears lives apart the same way every time: the ones who stayed die of it. The ones who left forget.

Kuo viskas baigiasi, jeigu ne tuo, kad išsilaipini krante kur apsnigtas tavo laivas. Grįžti į tą kitą pasaulį, kuriame viskas neaišku ir atsitiktinumas, nėra tavo šeimos, nėra tavo genties.

How it ends: you step onto a shore, your boat under snow. Back in the other world – no certainty, no family, no tribe.

Mirtis nebus nugalėta, po didele tuopa pasiklojęs, užmigo budriai, naktimis staiga pakeldamas galvą.

Death will not be conquered; having made his bed beneath a tall poplar, he fell asleep alert, lifting his head suddenly in the night.

Public letter to friends in Norway · 8 November (Norwegian original + translation)

Read Norwegian original · Norsk original+

Mange av dere skriver til meg. Dette er det som skjedde.

Først: Har jeg gjort deg urett, beklager jeg.

8. november ble jeg utvist fra Norge og sendt til Litauen.

Jeg sonet dommen min. Jeg møtte selv til soning. Jeg dyttet én mann og presset ham mot en vegg. Jeg drepte ikke. Jeg stjal ikke. Jeg ranet ikke. Jeg prøvde å unngå alt dette så godt jeg kunne.

Det finnes en vold loven knapt ser: å bli krenket med ord, vist forakt og behandlet dårlig av en som tror du ikke kan ta igjen eller stå på ditt. Den kjenner jeg godt. Jeg sier det som et faktum, ikke som et forsvar.

Papirene kom mens jeg satt i fengsel. De ble lest opp av en litauisk tolk jeg ikke hadde bedt om og knapt forsto. Ingen kunne forklare forskjellen på fristen på 72 timer og klagefristen på tre uker. De ansatte visste ikke hva UDI-papirene mine betydde. Ringekortet ga bare opptattsignal. Cellekameraten min, selv advokat, gjentok at hans beste og sterkeste advokat tok seg av saken. Jeg trodde på ham for lenge.

Siste dag ble jeg stoppet i porten og satt inn i en varebil. Flere ganger fikk jeg høre at vi skulle til Trondheim. Vi kom til Trandum. Rundt åtte politifolk sto oppstilt ved døren.

Tre dager på internatet. To timer til å skrive hvorfor jeg burde få bli. Avslag samme kveld.

Tre menn fulgte meg til Vilnius. Lederen deres behandlet meg med den største respekt. Han bød på kaffe. Ved avskjeden sa han stille, på engelsk: «You are not wrong.» Det er jeg takknemlig for å ha hørt, selv om han kan ha grunn til å benekte det.

Saken advokaten min skulle sende inn, ble aldri sendt inn.

Jeg har klaget. Vedtaket behandler meg som en trussel mot grunnleggende samfunnshensyn. For meg er tanken grotesk. UDI tok ikke hensyn til behandlingen jeg var i, eller til livet jeg hadde bygget i Norge. Sjansene er små. Det vet jeg.

Jeg klandrer ingen. Jeg er takknemlig for alt.

Jeg har det bra. Her i Litauen jobber jeg mot nye mål: fullføre universitetet, bli paramedisiner, bli bedre i norsk, komme tilbake. Jeg er invitert til å snakke for arkitektstudenter. Det løfter meg, og jeg er takknemlig. Arbeidet med sosiale boliger fortsetter.

Materielle ting betyr ingenting for meg. Jeg er stolt av å komme tilbake som den jeg er. Det jeg savner, er vennene mine.

Ville jeg tilbake i dag, var det av tre grunner: å klemme hver og en av dere, å reise fritt og snakke for studenter, å møte menneskene jeg skriver med om boligene.

Jeg holder vennene mine høyt: måtene vi møttes på, tiden vi hadde sammen.

Til alle jeg møtte i Norge: Det er en ære å kjenne dere. Vennligheten deres, klokskapen, og at dere tok meg imot som en av deres egne.

Snart inviterer jeg dere alle til det jeg skaper. Vi skal feire livet sammen.

Mantas

Many of you are writing to me. This is what happened.

First: if I have wronged you, I am sorry.

On 8 November I was deported from Norway and sent to Lithuania.

I served my sentence. I reported to serve it myself. I pushed one man and pressed him against a wall. I did not kill. I did not steal. I did not rob. I tried to avoid all of this as well as I could.

There is a violence the law barely sees: being degraded with words, shown contempt, and treated badly by someone who believes you cannot answer back or stand your ground. I know it well. I state it as fact, not defence.

The papers arrived while I was in prison. They were read aloud by a Lithuanian interpreter I had not asked for and barely understood. No one could explain the difference between the 72-hour deadline and the three-week appeal deadline. The staff did not know what my UDI papers meant. The phone card gave only a busy signal. My cellmate, himself a lawyer, kept repeating that his best and strongest lawyer was handling the case. I believed him too long.

On the last day I was stopped at the gate and placed in a van. Several times I was told we were going to Trondheim. We arrived at Trandum. About eight police officers stood lined up by the door.

Three days in the detention centre. Two hours to write why I should be allowed to stay. Refusal the same evening.

Three men escorted me to Vilnius. Their leader treated me with the utmost respect. He offered coffee. At parting he said quietly, in English: “You are not wrong.” I am grateful to have heard that, even if he may have reason to deny it.

The filing my lawyer was supposed to submit was never submitted.

I have appealed. The decision treats me as a threat to fundamental societal interests. To me the idea is grotesque. UDI took no account of the treatment I was in, nor of the life I had built in Norway. The chances are small. I know that.

I blame no one. I am grateful for everything.

I am well. Here in Lithuania I am working toward new goals: finishing university, becoming a paramedic, improving my Norwegian, returning. I have been invited to speak to architecture students. That lifts me, and I am grateful. The work on social housing continues.

Material things mean nothing to me. I am proud to return as I am. What I miss are my friends.

If I wanted to go back today, it would be for three reasons: to hug each one of you, to travel freely and speak to students, to meet the people I write to about the housing.

I hold my friends high: the ways we met, the time we had together.

To everyone I met in Norway: it is an honour to know you. Your kindness, your wisdom, and that you welcomed me as one of your own.

Soon I will invite you all to what I am building. We will celebrate life together.
